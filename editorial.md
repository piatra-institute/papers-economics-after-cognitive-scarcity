# Editorial record

Status: full read completed against the manuscript hashed in editorial-review.json.

## Contribution and reader

For anyone arguing in good faith about work and automation who has not noticed
that their conclusion depends on an unstated premise. The paper names that
premise, the permanent-remainder assumption, and computes what would have to be
true for it to hold. Confidence is high for the three benchmarks, which are
arithmetic under stated assumptions, and deliberately absent for any empirical
claim: there are no prices, no investment and no calibration anywhere in it.

## Material worth preserving

The cancellation of the arrival rate. It is the paper's least intuitive result
and the one that does the most work: an argument resting on how much new work
appears is answering a question that does not bear on the share it claims to
protect. The 84.85 percent figure, because conceding that the human-fallback
intuition is usually right is what makes the remaining region worth taking
seriously. The 50 percent containment crossing, because it reaches Bainbridge's
own recommendation by arithmetic rather than by disagreeing with her. And the
71 percent selection share, because it converts a rhetorical objection into a
control someone can actually run.

## Reverse outline

1. *Introduction* — the planner analogy, the Lucas critique, prior work on machine-dominated production, and the counterfactual with its four exclusions.
2. *Distribution under identical intelligence* — the two-good equilibrium, the three pooling rules, the support check, and the tenfold-output case.
3. *The permanent-remainder assumption* — the invalid inference, the task model and its limit, the nonempty frontier, the cancellation of the arrival rate, tasks with non-cognitive requirements, and the income share.
4. *Human fallback after automated incident response* — Bainbridge's concern, the fallback comparison, the independence requirement, the containment crossing, and the case-mix confound.
5. *Constraints that free cognition does not remove* — impossibility results, mechanisms that fail at zero cost, and the choice of objectives.
6. *Implications and limitations* — the status of the benchmarks, the target of the argument, concrete tests, and the implication of the allocation result.

## Strongest rival

That the counterfactual is too strong to be informative: nothing is ever free,
so results derived at exactly zero say nothing about a world of merely cheap
cognition. This has real force, and section 5 concedes half of it by showing a
mechanism whose equilibrium exists at every positive cost and fails at zero,
which is an argument for treating the limit as a different object rather than as
an extrapolation. The paper's answer is that the counterfactual is a device for
finding which constraints are being smuggled back in, and that its results are
statements about which arguments are incomplete rather than about what will
happen. A second rival is that task counts are the wrong unit; this is conceded
in claims.yaml as an assumption, with the reason the paper does not price them.

## Decisions and remaining checks

Argument: the Endsley and Kiris sentence originally attributed a specific
experimental finding. The full text could not be obtained, so the sentence was
rewritten to assert only what the title and subject establish, and the claim
ledger records why. This is a substantive weakening and the right one.

Structure: the concession that the human is usually the right fallback was moved
ahead of the counterexample, because the section is worthless if it reads as
denying the ironies-of-automation argument rather than bounding it.

Voice: ten editorial candidates were reported; four were rewritten as positive
declaratives where the rejected alternative was not a position anyone holds, and
six were kept because the paper's subject is exactly the difference between a
sound argument and its over-extension, so both halves of those contrasts matter.
Four spelled quantities reporting measurements were left as words where they
name a range rather than a measurement.

Verified separately: three source claims carry verification notes in claims.yaml,
one of which records a retrieval failure and the resulting weakening of the text;
execution is in verification/benchmarks.json; all nine pages were inspected and
recorded in visual-review.json.

Remaining: the income-share question is bounded and not answered, and the paper
says so. A model with prices would be a separate piece of work.
